A Political Diary from October
Web Log 261115

I am returning to blogging here for the end of this year, I have missed it. This entry will be a political diary of October, particularly focusing on the rhetoric produced at the Labour Party Conference in late September. In line with the diary form, there will also be some amount of personal writing alongside this more analytical strand, in an attempt to find ways in which our conjuncture cuts through and enters everyday life.

I wrote much of this text closer to when this topic was more pressing news. Subsequent actions of Burnham's government have been much more dispiriting than his speech gave reason believe. Regardless, this diary asks a fundamental question of his analysis of 'ending neoliberalism' in this country, and so it is still worth engaging with the bold pronouncement he made.
.....

My job, in a wood and metal workshop producing furniture for the hospitality industry, is slow at present. We are pulling together a large order of trolleys for a global bank, and so tasks come through in big lumps. In between those big lumps of tasks, there is more dead time to fill with general maintenance and snag list jobs. I find this tiring, and would rather be straightforwardly busy.

I think often about whether I am able to sense fluctuations in the economy through my work. Through other sources I know that other companies in the woodworking industry are struggling. Where I work seems to be doing well, and are expanding. Our primary customers are high end restaurants, which are continuing to open and redesign their interiors with expensive furniture. This does not seem feasible for most restaurants I encounter, but clearly there is a strong clientele of monied foodies willing to dine at these new ventures. Or perhaps my boss is simply doing a classically good job of marketing, finding customers, and growing the business. It's hard to know for sure, but I suspect it to be a small sign of our increasingly K-shaped economy.

There is a plan in action to reinvigorate the Labour party. At his Labour conference speech, new Prime Minister Andy Burnham disavowed neoliberalism, the economy orthodoxy that has governed Britain's political system since its reaction to the crisis of the 1970s. By expressing his analysis of our problems in this way, and from them making a gallant promise, he hopes to reanimate us, the jaded masses.

The exact claim he makes reads: "I am ready to do things my predecessors wouldn’t. I will make a break with the direction of the past 40 years and put Britain on a new path. Build a new economy and a new politics." He lists the symptomatic problems as "Deindustrialisation. Deregulation. Privatisation. Austerity. Brexit." and alleges power has become centralised away from most people.

He is signalling the necessity of a clear break. Primarily, he demonstrates what this would mean by speaking to the nation's ability to proactively work towards it's own interests, through the welfare state and other aspects of domestic policy. But domestic policy is not the only apparatus and avenue neoliberalism works with or within. It also affects global relations.

In essence, this refers to the economic doctrine of dissolving boundaries and restrictions between nation states, allowing the free flow of goods and capital. By 'unleashing' the market to move through their borders unimpeded, the places participating in this flow will improve their economic prosperity. This is whether the liberalising state is competing to produce physical goods (often developing countries, making use of their lower wages or more lenient environmental regulations and accepting foreign investment to industrialise), or if they host multinational corporations, provide business services to coordinate those capital flows, and act as the primary consumer of those cheap goods. Neoliberal hegemony reached its zenith after the fall of the USSR, as most all countries were integrated into one world market, and continued to persist after the global financial crash, as central banks kept interest rates low to spur capital to flow faster around the world. But by many accounts, its orthodoxy is now on the wane.

So if Andy Burnham is promising to break away from neoliberalism, is he able to maintain this analysis for Britain's role in a shifting world-system?

Many attempts have been made to describe this apparent new phase of geopolitical relations, as the rise of developmentalist and populist governments have shifted policy away from liberalisation and globalisation towards varied interpretations of protectionism. Several analyses of these trends were compiled and reviewed by Benjamin Kunkel, co-founder of the journal n+1, in the July/August '26 edition of New Left Review, as an article titled 'The New National Question'.

In this piece, Kunkel uses the term 'economic nationalism' to describe the approach that many countries are moving towards, away from neoliberal globalisation. This is a broad moniker, which can take on left or right wing connotations, but generally it describes a rejection of the presumption of the benefits of globalisation, instead prioritising economic arrangements that directly favour the state's productive capacity, rather than on its ability to gel within wider material processes and relationships.

Kunkel analyses the perspective of a series of books, assessing their depictions of the causes and symptoms of this phenomenon. I won't describe these in detail here, they range from the rise of China to the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, but each set out a clear pattern of increased suspicion of international cooperation and its economic upside.

Presently, these ideas have succeeded electorally mainly via forceful right wing populists, such as Erdogan or Trump. Kunkel attempts to chart a path for how this shift could be used for the left, to build social democratic and socialist futures. This will indeed necessitate limiting the ability of capital to act in its own interest around the world without friction. Crucially, Kunkel also makes clear that if social democracy is to be built again from the ground up, it will be in a different ecological context than when the postwar consensus emerged, with significantly more pressure on resources and a necessity for green infrastructures.

For this analysis, the most useful definition Kunkel provides is that the driving popular force behind the end of neoliberalism is a widespread desire for the 'repoliticisation of the economy'. A program that truly achieves this can come from the left, as the right will forever have to align with some section of capital, even if they disavow others on nationalist grounds.

Andy Burnham's speech, despite vowing a break with neoliberalism, did little to position Britain in these shifting dynamics. It presented a vision of a stronger social democracy, cradle to grave welfare (most particularly the last years before the grave), but his few references to politics outside of the UK were brief and boilerplate.

The Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey was more elaborative on this point in his conference speech. His was an attempt to articulate a developmentalist future for the country, with mentions of reindustrialisation and public procurement strategy, in the hope of returning good British jobs without the public investment that might threaten fiscal rules.

As Foreign Secretary, Ed Miliband had to make more detailed reference to their geopolitical approach. Nothing especially clear or ground-breaking was announced, but he did make an affirmation of closer relations with the EU, as did Burnham. As they look under the sofa cushions for potential sources of economic growth, they have decided to brush against the wounds of Brexit, and to build consensus for a warmer agreement.

As affirmed in a feature from the FT in April, making this a reality is not as simple as declaring that it would be in our interest. Starmer's government also tried to ease relations, but were rebuffed except for minor agreements, due to their unwillingness to concede on key issues like the customs union or rejoining the single market - the EU will reject 'cherry picking' in any new agreement.

There is an EU-UK summit to take place in late November. Throughout October, Burnham has reasserted a commitment to improve relations to Commons and through the press, even refusing to rule out a referendum on rejoining the common market in an interview on the 25th. They are prodding at the post-Brexit political orthodoxy of avoiding any suggestion of a rehash, so this is one place we can see a break with Starmer materialising.

However, the EU has been held up by many as exemplifying neoliberal structures of free movement of capital and labour, alongside the WTO, and particular country-to-country trade deals such as NAFTA. If you hope to promote faster growth in the UK economy, you would rather have stronger links to (still) our largest trading partners. But if you are looking to break with neoliberalism, towards a politics of economic nationalism, rejoining or partnering with the EU would not be indicative of this goal.

Seen from a wider perspective however, perhaps improved cooperation with Europe would provide more autonomy to ease out of our role as Jeeves to the USA's blustering Wooster. I won't elaborate further on the historical dynamics of the 'special relationship', but safe to say that we remain very much on the hock to support the whims of the largest economy in the West. In all three speeches mentioned, there were affirmations of support for Ukraine, and a continual positioning of Iran as a threat to the UK itself. This was also a feature of a recent annual address from the director general of MI5 Ken Maccullum. Regardless of your standpoint on the morality or viability of these two conflicts, they do align with the foreign policy of the US bloc.

The one moment that seemed to stand at least partially counter to this was Miliband (his words were later referenced by Burnham), when he stated that this Labour government's actions in Gaza had been wrong. This differs from prior commitments of support for Israel in the 'war in Gaza', lightly checked by cautions about human rights and the importance of the two-state solution.

This signal has brought forth little action. Labour have instead spent quite a lot of energy attempting to frame an internal motion by the Green Party declaring that 'Zionism Is Racism' as antisemitic, even forsaking real political capital by ending power-sharing in with the party in certain council seats. They have maintained the proscription of Palestine Action, choosing the absorb the attention brought by the protest of bestselling author Sally Rooney, and subsequent withdrawal of her books from shelves. At present this is not a shift that they are delivering on. They have admitted being wrong previously, and are committing to continuing to be wrong.

Continued alignment with the US bloc is also apparent from another clear priority: : increasing military spending, to reach NATO target of 3.5% of GDP by 2035. Healey even intimated that this mobilisation could form the basis of coherent post-neoliberal domestic economic policy: reindustrialisation via militarisation. This strategy would look for a devolution of power and economic prosperity to areas outside of London, by building drone factories in old pit villages.

I recently saw a splashy YouTube video produced by the VC-backed firm Isembard, who have opened a factory in Southwark to supply companies such as Anduril with productive capacity. They're operating under the tagline 'Reindustrialise The West', to be extra sure that their Clash of Civilizations rhetoric is understood.

Andrea Egan, head of Unison trade union, rejects this vision in an article in Tribune from August. In our current predicament of zero-sum budgeting, in which all increases in spending must be paid for with cuts from elsewhere, almost all arguments for increasing defence spending come at the expense of cutting welfare. She points out that our military spending is already the sixth highest in the world, and that the objectives reinforced by this spending are all determined by the United States. Rejecting neoliberalism and realigning economic policy towards the interests of your own nation doesn't suggest that cutting welfare spending in favour of supporting another countries military goals is a priority.

Egan was recently elected to Unison as the furthest left candidate to lead a national trade union for a long time, and unfortunately her rejection of military industrialism is not shared by all other unions - many see the prospect as likely to provide more jobs for their members.

A recent report from the Transition Security Project questions the military logic of our current defence spending, and its slated increase, as they argue that large amounts of our present budget is used on force projection, whereas all of the posed threats on British shores from abroad would require protection at home. Arguments for increased spending argue for improved defence, but would result only in more capacity to support American global ambitions and escapades.

If this is bargain we are being forced to accept by NATO and the US in its role as hegemon, what other options exist for positioning the UK globally? Herein perhaps lies the counterintuitive logic of moving towards increased economic nationalism by improving ties to the EU.

In an interview on The Rest is Politics podcast, hosted by the dual Gríma Wormtongues for British centrist dads of Rory Stewart and Alistair Campbell, Burnham is questioned on his opinion of Canadian prime minister Mark Carney's 'middle powers strategy'. This formulation was put forward by Carney in a speech to the World Economic Forum in January. He describes some of the ways the global order is changing, particularly the changing role of the United States. Carney's election in 2025 was hugely influenced by his defiant response to Trump's suggestion of US annexation of Canada.

At Davos, in the wake of the US' capture of Nicolas Maduro in Venezuela, it seemed clear that the US, rather than the isolationism espoused in Trump's first term, was to take a more outwardly aggressive posture, particularly to its neighbours in the Western Hemisphere. In response, Carney proposed that 'middle powers' caught between the two hegemons of the US and China worked together as a bloc. This is includes European nations, India, Japan, and others. The result would be that nations who have up to now remained consensual to the US as sole global hegemon would begin to assert their own autonomy as part of a wider coalition.

When asked about this suggestion by Rory Stewart, Burnham isn't hugely responsive, and leans towards a fairly general line that the UK will keep 'options open to any partners'. When pressed on Britain's relationship to the US, in particular over the issue of a planned televised execution, he continued to express neutrality and continuity.

We seem to be continually bound to the 'special relationship'. There are a series of structural economic factors that tie us tightly together, for example the liquidity swap lines our central bank receives from the Fed, or how London plays host to the Eurodollar market through which petrodollars are recycled. This list could of course be greatly extended. As twin architects of the financialised post-industrial future concocted in the 1980s, we have knitted a grand web of inter-reliant financial services upon which we expect our economy to rely. I fear that this is not accounted for in the promise to 'break with neoliberalism'. Neoliberalism is not just an ideology that we have subscribed to, it is what we do. And we have done it in partnership with a perishing empire.

I say this not to dissuade from the possibility of breaking with the US. It has been morally imperative for a long time, but it is clearly an economic necessity now also. We can no longer act as puppet state, at pain of remaining hitched to their spiralling global death cult and the neoliberal economic logics that have become so unworkable that even the USA is leaving behind. Their present adventurism is designed to ensure that other smaller allied and non-allied countries remain neoliberal, in that they're open to American capital markets. Economic nationalism for me but not for thee. To break from the strictures of neoliberalism is to break from the United States.

Politics continues to feel stuck, despite the promised breaks. It is hard to make historical breaks. There are real entrenched power structures that can convey their interests. We are, famously at this point, beholden to the bond markets. Or at least, it is common political orthodoxy, in the wake of the Truss affair, that government cannot promote radical policy or the cost of future borrowing will sharply increase. This dynamic, whether real or exaggerated, is the keenest example of the economic nationalist question facing Britain. A repoliticisation and democratisation of the economy would deal return the priorities of the people above those of the bond markets. The tactics needed the refute the strength of their influence is still an open question on the left. The economist Daniela Gabor blames the quantitative tightening perpetrated by the Bank of England, and makes the case for its independence to be rescinded. The impacts of the Office for Budget Responsibility is also under intense scrutiny. Answering these questions of who holds economic legitimacy is another primary political priority for moving to a post-neoliberal age.

It isn't clear to me how a developmentalist Britain will approach these problems, other than continuing its perpetual drift around tired loops, even if furnished with new rhetoric, at least finally using the right term to name its problems. There is to be a '10 Year Plan' released in mid-December, perhaps this will bring more clarity for Labour's intended direction. There is a 'Modern Industrial Strategy' published last June, prior to Burnham's premiership, but indicative at least of what the party expect to be the fundamentals of our economy. The Autumn Budget has provided little further indication. It was decidedly fiddly, with gestures towards inflation relief, emphasis on military spending, and a small windfall tax on banks. The closest suggestion of an economic nationalist turn were changes to public procurement policy, to incentivise directing government spending towards British businesses. Are they too constrained by other powerful actors to make the radical steps they declared necessary? Or does their analysis stop short of substantially reorganising the economy?

My habitual presumption that the loops our politics have been caught in, the helter-skelter of managed decline, will continue. As always, the question is socialism or barbarism. The assertion to make a break in the former direction was received positively. It was made in a world that, due to a confluence of crises, seems to be posing that same question all over simultaneously. As Kunkel puts it in his NLR article, the prospect of economic nationalism is an affirmation of the power of the nation state form - but this is not necessarily power for working or even middle classes. In our financialised and post-Fordist economy, it isn't straightforward to define what the 'means of production' are in Britain. But it remains the case that bringing about their acquisition and distribution to the working class is our goal. The balance of forces against that proposition must be met with guile and force. Burnham has expressed a desire to return democratic and economic power to British people. He is not a socialist by any means, but will he be able to achieve even his modest goals from our contradictory position in the world-system? Does he understand what it is that he promised? I am not sure. I hope the loop can be broken.
